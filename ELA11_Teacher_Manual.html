1. How This Course Works

A typical lesson

Every literature and grammar/vocab lesson (e.g. M1_W1_L04) follows the same shell: navy header → a four-box metadata strip (Quarter/Module, Week/Lesson, Topic, Word Study) → a gold "Today's Focus" framing box → three numbered "by the end of this lesson" objectives → the core teaching content with one HTML/CSS visual (a sentence-combining board, a labeled diagram, a period artwork) → an ungraded self-check widget (click an option, get feedback, reset button) → a 📓 Reading Journal badge with up-front coaching (never a hidden answer) → the Word Study table (now four columns: word, in-context quote, meaning, and word origin) → a second Reading Journal badge for the vocabulary task → "What's Next" → a closing "Carry This Forward" line → footer. Nothing on this page is graded; the graded touchpoint is always a separate Discussion, Feeder, or Recitation linked from the week's Module Map.

2. Ungraded Interactives & the Intro/Outro Wrappers

Ungraded interactive widgets (the click-to-select self-checks on every literature/grammar lesson) produce no submission and cost you nothing to grade. They are formation-by-practice; a student who skips them shows up indirectly, in weaker discussion posts or a thinner draft, not as a missing grade in your gradebook.

Intro/outro wrappers are Canvas Quizzes in survey mode, not wiki pages — one per module, opening and closing it. Each captures: the essential question (first answer, then revised), a confidence rating (Likert 1–5, outro only), "what's still unclear," a virtue reflection, and (outro) a completion reminder for the Quarterly Reading Check. As of 2026-07-14 there is no group-work opt-in question on any wrapper — every wrapper explicitly notes "the project is solo." The single highest-leverage move in this course: spend 15–20 minutes per module skimming outro responses for low-confidence clusters and repeated "unclear" items, then fold what you find into your next announcement (see Section 9).

New this session: a Project Pitch Discussion now runs once per module (M1 Wk7, M2 Wk7–8) — see Section 7 for the full spec. It is graded (Tier 2, 10 pts), not an ungraded wrapper, but it is designed the same way: low teacher-authoring burden, high signal about where students are before the crunch week.

3. AI & VR in This Course (Student-Facing)

AI

Ordinary weekly lessons use generic, factual "an AI tool can/cannot…" callouts — consistent with the school's AI-awareness-not-required-tool-use posture. Three places name Claude specifically, and you should know why so you can explain the difference if a student asks: Module 0's two "Working with Claude" orientation pages (ungraded setup, built entirely around Claude Code), and the narrow "check your rhythm/verify a fact, never rewrite" step embedded in both capstone project workshops (Elsinore Stage, Publius Returns). New exception (2026-07-14): the Publius Broadside companion deliberately lets Claude Code write actual HTML/CSS, because that assignment's task is building a webpage. The one line that must hold in all cases: the student's own argument/scene text is never generated or altered by AI — only the code/presentation layer is, and only in that one assignment.

Disclosure mechanism throughout: a 4-column AI-use log (What I asked / What it gave me / What I kept / What was wrong) in the Module Organizer, referenced again at both capstone Workshop/Draft/Final stages and the Broadside.

VR

VR appears only as an optional recording-location choice — never a required or VR-specific activity. Students may record their graded recitation, the optional Company Gallery reading (M1), or the optional Civic Address (M2) "in a plain space or in a virtual setting of your choice." No ENGAGE/Locations/Sessions vocabulary appears anywhere; captions/transcripts make every recording gradable without a headset.

4. Using Ignite AI to Support Students (Teacher-Facing)

[VERIFY] before relying on this section: Ignite AI's exact feature names and availability vary by Canvas release and by what Optima has enabled on this instance. Confirm the current feature set before promising a specific button to a new facilitator.

Used well, Ignite AI (Canvas's teacher-facing AI assistant) is what makes a 500:1 ratio feel personal. In this course specifically:

  • Drafting individualized feedback on Discussion posts and the two capstone Finals — ask it to draft a comment from your rubric notes, then you edit and own every word before posting.
  • Summarizing outro-wrapper and discussion analytics — feed it the confidence ratings and "what's unclear" responses from an intro/outro wrapper or a Project Pitch discussion and ask for the cohort's top 2–3 sticking points, to fold into your next announcement.
  • Surfacing students who need a nudge — a string of low confidence ratings, a missed Reading Journal Check, or a thin recitation rehearsal note are all signals; ask Ignite AI to draft a warm, specific check-in message, which you then personalize and send.
  • Generating practice variants for a student still shaky on subordination, an appositive, or MLA citation before the next grammar lesson locks it in.
  • Family communication — especially useful drafting the advance notices in Section 13 for the Douglass Narrative and Hamlet's Act III–V content.

Standing rule: AI drafts, the teacher decides. Never let it rank, surveil, or auto-penalize a student; every output is read, edited, and owned by you before it reaches a student or family.

7. Discussions, Recitations & Assessment Activities — How to Run & Grade

By type

  • Weekly literature Discussion (10 pts, Tier 2): post by midweek, reply to 1 classmate by week's end. Read in a batch; drop 2–3 public model comments (see Section 8).
  • Project Pitch Discussion (10 pts, Tier 2, new): post an elevator pitch, reply to at least 2 classmates. Skim for students still choosing a vague gap/claim and redirect before the crunch week.
  • Feeder (10–15 pts, Tier 1–2): short scaffolding piece, 2–3 criterion checklist.
  • Recitation (ungraded rehearsal most weeks; 20 pts graded once per module, Tier 2): grade from the audio; face optional; caption/transcript confirms accuracy.
  • Module Project (50 pts, Tier 3, once per module): the course's only substantive teacher-graded item each module; ~5 min/student with the rubric.
  • The Publius Broadside (10 pts, Tier 1, new): a checklist item, ~1 min/student — confirm the op-ed text is unchanged, don't grade web-design quality.
  • Reflection wrappers (intro/outro, completion): mine in aggregate, 15–20 min/cohort (Section 2).

8. Coach, Not Catch

  • Feedback names the next step, not just the error; lead with what's working.
  • Tier 1 feeders and self-checks allow re-attempts — they are practice, not a trap.
  • Use outro/pitch-discussion analytics to find students who need help early, then reach out; never to build a case against them.
  • Suspected AI misuse (an unusually polished pitch, an argument that doesn't sound like the student's earlier drafts): open a conversation, ask the student to talk through their thinking, treat it as formation, not an accusation.
  • Public discussion comments model good thinking; they never single out weak work for correction in public.

12. What Makes This Course Classical & Cultivating Culture

What makes it classical

  • Primary-source encounter, not summary: students read Hamlet, the Federalist, "Self-Reliance," and Douglass's Narrative in full or substantial excerpt, grounded against stable public-domain texts, never a paraphrase.
  • The Great Conversation, made explicit: Module 2's Week 4 bridge lesson names the American throughline directly — the founders, Emerson, and Douglass as one long argument about self-government across time.
  • Imitatio: both module projects ask students to write in the mode of what they studied (blank verse for Hamlet, a pseudonymous Federalist-style op-ed), not just write about it.
  • Disputatio-adjacent structure: the claim-concede-counter move (Feeder 3, Publius Returns) is a formal argument discipline, not a five-paragraph-essay formula.
  • The virtue thread: self-government is named explicitly in both modules' outro wrappers and tied to something concrete (memorizing/drafting on your own schedule; conceding the other side honestly).

Cultivating culture, asynchronously

  • Public discussion comments that name good thinking build a real intellectual community even without live class time.
  • Weekly announcements that reference actual student work (Section 9) model that you're reading closely, not broadcasting.
  • The virtue reflection in every outro wrapper keeps the course's moral seriousness visible without moralizing at students.
  • The new Project Pitch discussions are, among other things, a culture-building move: students see each other's work-in-progress and practice giving real feedback, which is otherwise rare in a fully solo, async course.

14. Adapting to a Live, 4-Day/Week Section

Note: you asked for "two things" to be added to this manual but described one (this section). I've built the live-adaptation guidance below; if there was a second addition you intended, let me know and I'll add it.

The async build already separates content (lessons) from graded touchpoints (discussions, feeders, recitations, projects) cleanly enough that a 4-day/week live section can reuse it almost entirely — the main job is redistributing four lessons/activities across four class meetings instead of a self-paced week, and using class time for what async students do alone. Below is a suggested weekly pattern for an ordinary week (Weeks 1–6 of either module); heavy weeks (recitation, project weeks) are noted separately.

DayIn an ordinary week, do this in class
Day 1Cover Lesson L01 together: read the assigned scene/chapter aloud or in parts, then walk the lesson's core visual and self-check as a class discussion instead of solo clicking. Assign L02 as pre-reading for Day 2.
Day 2Discuss L02's content, then run that week's Canvas Discussion prompt as a live Socratic conversation (students still post their answer to Canvas afterward for the grade, but the "reply to a classmate" happens live in class instead).
Day 3Cover L03, then do the L04 grammar/vocab mini-lesson together (it is short by design and works well as a 15-minute direct-instruction segment). Use remaining time for Reading Journal work time with the teacher circulating.
Day 4Work-and-conference day: students draft/finish that week's Feeder in class with the teacher available for questions; the teacher can informally check Reading Journal progress and flag anyone falling behind before the week closes.

Weeks with a recitation or a project stage

  • Recitation weeks (M1 Wk7, M2 Wk7): use one full class day as a live "recitation day" — students perform in person rather than recording solo; the teacher scores live using the same 3-criterion rubric. This is explicitly allowed as "Option B" in both recitation specs.
  • Project Pitch weeks: run the pitch-and-feedback discussion live instead of on Canvas — students give 30–60 second pitches to the room and get feedback from at least two classmates on the spot, then post a one-line summary to Canvas for the grade record.
  • Draft/Workshop weeks (M1 Wk8, M2 Wk8 pre-Final): the Workshop lesson's Claude-Code step and the drafting itself both work well as an in-class workshop day, with the teacher conferencing student-to-student while they draft.
  • Final weeks (M1 Wk9, M2 Wk8 Final): keep as independent revision/writing time, either in class with the teacher circulating or as homework with a check-in day, since this is individual, quiet work.

In every case, the underlying assignments, rubrics, and Canvas gradebook setup in Sections 5–7 stay identical between the On-Demand and Live sections — only the pacing and delivery of the ungraded lesson content changes, matching the syllabus's own "same content and expectations, different pacing" framing.
